    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner’s Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 5 Reactivity, section 5.2 Using the reactivity series and displacement reactions, pages 166–170; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 89–91. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The Getting started partner paper game (building the series name by name) is a classroom activity; here step 1 checks the two ends of the series instead. The written planning task (Part 1 of Identifying a mystery metal: equipment list, method, and what each test tells you) is classroom writing; the investigation itself — displacement tests, then the deciding zinc sulfate test — is steps 2–3 of this lesson, with the book’s own result pattern (displaces iron and copper, not magnesium; candidates aluminium and zinc).

    Objectives (from the book): use displacement reactions to identify an unknown metal; learn about some useful displacement reactions. Key words: blast furnace, molten, ores, thermite reaction — plus reactivity series, displacement, coke and exothermic.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the models on the 3D lab bench itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark ‘Show me’ line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. ‘Show me how’ only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the ‘On the bench’ panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the model on the bench. The steps follow the book’s order: the reactivity series first, then the mystery-metal investigation (the book’s Think like a scientist task, with its safety point — an unknown metal is handled with forceps), then the thermite reaction (aluminium + iron oxide → aluminium oxide + iron; iron molten at 1535 °C; lit by magnesium powder and barium nitrate; used to weld rails in situ), then displacement by carbon in the blast furnace (iron oxide + carbon → iron + carbon dioxide; known for about 3500 years), and finally the book’s copper oxide + charcoal experiment generalised into a sort of which oxides carbon can extract. The exercises follow Workbook 5.2: Focus — judging statements about the book’s mystery metal; Practice — the deciding test, extracting lead with carbon, the copper prediction and why carbon fails for aluminium; Challenge — sorting thermite and blast furnace facts from Workbook exercises 3 and 4.
