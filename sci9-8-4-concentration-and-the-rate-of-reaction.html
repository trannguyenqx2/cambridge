    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 8 Rates of reaction, section 8.4 Concentration and the rate of reaction, pages 278–287; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 163–170. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The section project (manufacturing carbon dioxide for sale) and the unit check-your-progress pages are classroom project and review tasks and are not part of this interactive lesson; the check-your-progress ideas are reused inside the exercises.

    Objectives (from the book): investigate the effect of concentration on the rate of reaction; plan an investigation; explain the effect of changes in concentration on the rate of reaction, using particle theory; learn about another way to speed up a reaction, using a catalyst. Key words: catalyst, catalase, enzymes, biological catalysts, decompose — plus concentration and collision.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the apparatus on the 3D chemistry bench itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the chemistry bench' panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the model on the bench. The steps follow the book's order: planning the marble-and-acid investigation from the Getting started discussion, making the book's own dilution table (solution A: 10 cm³ acid + 40 cm³ water, up to solution E: 50 cm³ acid + 0 cm³ water, each made up to 50 cm³), timing how long each solution takes to collect 25 cm³ of carbon dioxide and reading the book's typical-results graph (steeper line = faster reaction, same final volume of gas), explaining the pattern with particle theory (more acid particles in the same space, so collisions are more frequent), then the book's catalyst investigation — hydrogen peroxide decomposing slowly on its own, and much faster with manganese oxide, which is recovered unchanged and works again — ending with enzymes such as catalase, the biological catalysts in living cells. The exercises use the Workbook's own tasks: 8.4A planning Zara and Sofia's zinc-and-acid investigation, 8.4B Arun's results at concentrations ×2, ×3 and ×4 (and the unused ×0), and 8.4C collecting 100 cm³ of carbon dioxide as fast as possible with only 20 g of marble chips and 50 cm³ of standard acid.
