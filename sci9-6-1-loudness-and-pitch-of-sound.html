    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 6 Sound and space, section 6.1 Loudness and pitch of sound, pages 197–206; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 109–111. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The hands-on ruler investigation (clamping a metre rule, hanging masses, timing vibrations and plotting a graph) and the wave drawings of Workbook 6.1B are practical and pencil tasks: the investigation's ideas — counting vibrations in 10 seconds to get frequency, and how mass and length change frequency — are taught in step 5 and the exercises, and the drawings are practised by choosing the correct changed wave, but the physical practical itself is not part of this interactive lesson.

    Objectives (from the book): find out about the amplitude and the frequency of a sound wave; learn how amplitude is linked to loudness and how frequency is related to pitch; learn to recognise amplitude and frequency from a diagram of a sound wave. Key words: amplitude, frequency, loudness, oscilloscope, peak, pitch, trough.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the instruments on the 3D sound bench itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the bench' panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the instrument on the bench. The steps follow the book's order: every sound-maker vibrates; amplitude is the greatest distance particles move from rest, read from a peak or a trough, and a bigger amplitude means a louder sound; an oscilloscope with a microphone draws the wave; frequency counts vibrations each second in hertz (Hz), and a bigger frequency means a higher pitch; shorter vibrating lengths vibrate faster — the book's piano A notes double each octave (27.5, 55, 110, 220, 440 Hz). The exercises use the Workbook's own tasks: 6.1A comparing waves on an oscilloscope, 6.1B changing amplitude and frequency, and 6.1C the wave whose particles move 0.5 mm each way with 250 vibrations a second, and the change from amplitude 0.1 mm / 1200 Hz to 0.04 mm / 1500 Hz.
