    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 7 Genes and inheritance, section 7.4 Natural selection, pages 251–259; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 141–146. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The section project 'How we learnt about DNA' — a group research report contributing to a class timeline display — and the unit Check your progress pages are classroom research and review tasks and are not part of this interactive lesson.

    Objectives (from the book): learn about the scientific theory of natural selection; find out about some examples of natural selection in action. Key words: advantageous, feature, natural selection, resistant — plus variation, gene, offspring, generation, camouflage and antibiotic.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the models on the 3D selection bench itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the selection bench' panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the model on the bench. The steps follow the book's order: the Getting started waxy monkey leaf frog and the imaginary giraffe story (variation, the dry year, genes passed to offspring — the book's five-point summary of the theory), then the real evidence from bacteria and antibiotics, then the peppered moths (pale before 1849, dark by 1900, pale again today), and finally the book's Summary checklist as a sorting step. The exercises use the Workbook's own tasks: 7.4A blue-tailed lizards (where genes are found, what inheritance means, and which explanation of blue tails is correct), 7.4B the camouflaged-caterpillars pasta experiment (what to record, how to make the conclusion reliable, and the correct order of the five explanation sentences), and 7.4C using natural selection to explain how woolly mammoths developed from steppe mammoths.
