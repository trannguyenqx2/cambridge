    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 8 Rates of reaction, section 8.2 Surface area and the rate of reaction, pages 268–272; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 154–157. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The Getting started book-face measurement is discussed in the step conclusions and cube calculations rather than measured with a real book, and the real Bunsen-burner work (magnesium, iron and the marble-chip investigation with dilute hydrochloric acid) is simulated on the bench for safety — in class it needs safety glasses and teacher supervision.

    Objectives (from the book): investigate the rate of reaction when the surface area of one of the reactants is changed; carry out an investigation using the reaction between calcium carbonate and dilute hydrochloric acid; consider how changing the shape of a material can affect the surface area. Key word: surface area.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the models on the 3D reaction bench itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the reaction bench' panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the model on the bench. The steps follow the book's order: magnesium block, ribbon and powder (only surface atoms can react), then burning iron (nail, wool, filings), then the 27-cube surface-area calculations (large cube 54, flat 3 × 9 shape 78, separated cubes 162 square units), then the book's own marble-chip investigation (5 g of chips with 25 cm³ of acid, readings every 30 seconds), its results and the powdered-calcium-carbonate prediction, and a final sort. The exercises use the Workbook's own Zara investigation: flat zinc against a lump of zinc of the same mass in hydrochloric acid, the hydrogen collected over water, the graph whose two lines finish at the same total volume, and the collision-theory challenge, including the line expected for powdered zinc.
