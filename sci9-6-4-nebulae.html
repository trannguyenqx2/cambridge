    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 6 Sound and space, section 6.4 Nebulae, pages 221–223; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 121–124. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The Getting started group discussion (how the planets formed, and how planets and stars are alike and different) and Activity 6.4.1 (a virtual tour — researching nebula types and the Hubble Space Telescope online and designing an information sheet) are classroom talk and research tasks and are not part of this interactive lesson — except that comparing nebula types and the Hubble telescope are built into steps 2 and 6. The first pages of the Workbook pack for this section repeat the end of section 6.3 (the Moon capture theory) and are not part of this section, so they are not used here.

    Objectives (from the book): discover that nebulae are clouds of dust and gas in space; learn how stars can form in nebulae. Key words: nebulae (singular: nebula), northern hemisphere, southern hemisphere, stellar nurseries — plus hydrogen, helium and gravity.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the models on the 3D space stage itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the space stage' panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the model on the stage. The steps follow the book's order: what nebulae are (clouds of dust and gas, mostly hydrogen with a smaller quantity of helium, particles very far apart), the two nebulae easiest to see from each hemisphere (Orion in the north, Tarantula in the south — both without a telescope), size (the Orion nebula is 24 ly across against the Solar System's 0.001 ly — 24 000 times bigger; many nebulae are more than 10 000 times bigger than the Solar System), nebulae born from exploding giant stars, stellar nurseries (gravity, growing mass, pressure, heat, atoms reacting), and finally the four Workbook nebula types. The Workbook graph of star formation rate over the age of the universe (6.4C question 3) is a plotted-data description task whose plotted values are not in the text pack, so the Challenge exercise uses the same section's definition of star formation rate instead.
