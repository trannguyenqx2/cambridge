    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 1 Photosynthesis and the carbon cycle, section 1.4 Climate change, pages 28–41; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), section 1.4, pages 20–23. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. Not included in this interactive lesson: the section's group Project (researching and presenting what happened to the dinosaurs) and the end-of-unit Check your progress review — both are classroom research and review tasks. The book's Activity 1.4.1 (listing actions that raise or lower atmospheric carbon dioxide, to slow climate change) is a class discussion task and is represented here through the fossil-fuels point in Step 1 rather than as a separate step.

    Objectives: find out how the climate of the Earth has changed in the past; learn what happened when an asteroid collided with the Earth; consider how the increase in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere affects the climate today and in the future. Key words: slush, mass extinction, meteorites, meteoroids, meteors — plus greenhouse gas, carbon dioxide, climate, ice age, asteroid, fossil fuels and sea level.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the climate models on the 3D stage itself, sorting past-climate cards, or answering the sea-level calculation. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the stage' panel only move the camera; meeting a model means tapping it on the stage. The examples follow the book's order and values: the Getting started greenhouse diagram first, then past climates (first ice age about 2 billion years ago; snowball Earth about 650 million years ago), the asteroid collisions (470 and 67 million years ago; up to 75% of species extinct), meteoroid–meteor–meteorite and the 2006 DP14 risk example (200 m × 400 m; 2 400 000 km away in 2014), the Think like a scientist sea-level experiments, and finally today's change (2016 about 1 °C above 1880; sea level rising about 3 mm a year, so about 24 cm by the end of the century; more than 600 million people at risk). The exercises use the Workbook's three NASA/NOAA climate graphs (sea level, Bering Sea ice, carbon dioxide concentration).
