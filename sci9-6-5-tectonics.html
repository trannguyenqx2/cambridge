    For teachers and parents — about this lesson

    Source: Based on Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Learner's Book 9 (2nd edition, 2021; ISBN 9781108742863), Unit 6 Sound and space, section 6.5 Tectonics, pages 224–233; Workbook 9 (ISBN 9781108742894), pages 125–130. All wording in this lesson is rewritten in our own words; no text is copied from the book. The section project on impact craters and the unit check-your-progress pages are classroom investigation and review tasks and are not part of this interactive lesson. Activity 6.5.1 (cutting out paper continents to rebuild Pangaea) is represented here by the moving continent models in step 3 and the calculations in the exercises.

    Objectives (from the book): discover how convection currents cause the movement of tectonic plates; learn about the evidence we have for tectonic plates. Key words: tectonic plates, convection current — plus crust, mantle, core, fossil record, alignment and mid-oceanic ridge.

    How it works: each step asks for one real action — tapping the models on the 3D Earth bench itself, or sorting cards into groups. The lesson checks the action itself (the dark 'Show me' line), and the answer stays locked until the action is done. 'Show me how' only demonstrates — it never counts as done. Buttons in the 'On the Earth bench' panel only move the camera; testing an idea means tapping the model on the bench. The steps follow the book's order: the structure of the Earth from the Getting started discussion, then convection in the mantle driven by the core (over 5000 °C), plates moving slowly (0.6–10 cm per year in the book), then the evidence in the book's own order — the continental jigsaw and Pangaea (335–175 million years ago), Mesosaurus and Glossopteris fossils, magnetic alignment at mid-oceanic ridges (poles swapped almost 200 times in 100 million years), and earthquakes and volcanoes clustering at plate boundaries. The exercises use the Workbook's own tasks and numbers: 6.5A layers and convection arrows, 6.5B the mid-Atlantic ridge and the separation-speed calculation (5000 km in 140 million years ≈ 3.6 cm per year), and 6.5C evidence including GPS showing North America moving west at 23 mm per year.
